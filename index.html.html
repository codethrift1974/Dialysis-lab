Background & Pre-Lab Predictions

Dialysis tubing acts as a membrane model. Molecules move via down a without using cellular energy. Based on molecular formulas and size, predict which substances can cross the membrane pores:

Experimental Procedure (Handout Section 7)

Step 1: Set Up External Environment (250 mL Beaker) Pending

Pour 150 mL distilled water into the beaker, add 11 drops of Lugol's iodine, swirl until pale amber/yellow, and test a baseline drop on a glucose strip.

Step 2: Assemble Internal Environment (Dialysis Bag) Pending

Tie one end of pre-soaked 15 cm dialysis tubing, add 5 mL starch + 5 mL glucose, tie top end with small air gap, and rinse the outside under distilled water.

Step 3: Initiate Incubation Pending

Submerge the dialysis bag completely into the amber iodine solution and run the incubation timer.

Step 4: Post-Incubation Observation & Testing Pending

Lift the bag out of the beaker, observe color changes inside the bag and in the beaker, and test a drop from the bottom of the beaker on a new glucose test strip.

DOK 3

How do your experimental results relate to the molecular formulas and relative sizes of water (H2O), iodine (I2), glucose (C6H12O6), and starch (C300H520O260)? Were your pre-lab predictions accurate?

Sentence starter: "Comparing the chemical formulas shows that smaller molecules like ____ passed through the membrane pores, whereas ____..."
Look at the total number of atoms in each formula: H₂O (3 atoms), I₂ (2 atoms), and C₆H₁₂O₆ (24 atoms) vs. C₃₀₀H₅₂₀O₂₆₀ (1,080+ atoms). Relate that size difference to the microscopic pores in the tubing.
DOK 3

Explain how dialysis tubing serves as a model for biological cell membranes. What is one limitation of using plastic dialysis tubing to represent a living cellular membrane?

Sentence starter: "Dialysis tubing models a cell membrane because both are ____; however, one limitation is that living cell membranes ____..."
Think about what a living phospholipid bilayer has that inert polymer dialysis tubing lacks: transport protein channels/pumps, active transport using ATP, endocytosis/exocytosis, or dynamic responsiveness to signals.

1. Pre-Lab Predictions Key (Section 4)

  • Water (H2O): Can Pass (small polar molecule; moves via osmosis into the hypertonic bag)
  • Iodine (I2): Can Pass (small diatomic molecule; diffuses into bag)
  • Glucose (C6H12O6): Can Pass (small monosaccharide; diffuses out into beaker)
  • Starch (C300H520O260): Too Large (macromolecular polysaccharide; trapped inside bag)

3. Post-Lab Analysis Exemplars (Section 9)

  1. Molecules crossing membrane: Iodine (I2), glucose (C6H12O6), and water (H2O). Evidence: the inside of the bag turned blue-black (showing iodine entered), the beaker glucose strip turned positive (showing glucose exited), and the bag gained mass/turgor (showing water entered via osmosis).
  2. Molecules unable to cross: Starch (C300H520O260). Evidence: the beaker solution contained iodine yet never turned blue-black, proving starch remained trapped inside the dialysis tubing.
  3. Relationship to molecular size: Molecules with few atoms (H2O = 3 atoms, I2 = 2 atoms, C6H12O6 = 24 atoms) are smaller than the microscopic pores of the dialysis tubing and diffuse down their concentration gradients. Starch is a massive polysaccharide (~1,080+ atoms) physically blocked by pore size.
  4. Cell membrane model & limitation: Both dialysis tubing and plasma membranes are selectively permeable and allow passive diffusion down concentration gradients. However, dialysis tubing is a non-living polymer that sorts strictly by pore size—it lacks phospholipid bilayers, embedded transport proteins, active transport (ATP pumps), and endocytosis/exocytosis.
